1. Document Purpose

This document describes the design of Warpbound as it stands in the playable demo: its systems, rules, numbers, rooms, tools, and the reasoning behind each decision. Section 36 covers the full game the demo is building towards.

One thing to know before reading: the demo gives the player their end-game power on purpose. In the full game these abilities are earned over the whole adventure. The demo hands them out quickly to show what the player becomes by the end.

2. Game Overview

Title Warpbound
Genre 2D side-view metroidvania, with shoot-'em-up flight sections
Player character Namir, a jet pilot
Core mechanic The TPS — a throwable teleportation sphere
Demo content 22 rooms, 3 sphere types, 2 elemental swords, 6 enemy types, 2 bosses, 3 fast-travel capsules, a jet to repair, an 8-wave flight
Session length A short demo, replayable
Fail state None. Dying restarts the room with full health

3. High Concept

Teleport anywhere you want.

You throw a fragile sphere and warp to it. The sphere is your movement, your puzzle tool, and part of your combat. Every new type of sphere and every new layer opens more of the map. Then you repair your crashed jet and fly out in a shoot-'em-up, where your swords decide your beams.

4. Design Pillars

Freedom

The player can teleport whenever they want, play around with it, solve puzzles in different ways and approach combat differently. Movement freedom is the feeling the whole game is built on.

Exploration

The map rewards curiosity. Rooms you can't finish yet are remembered and revisited. Walls hide things, and destroying them reveals the hidden layer behind. Side rooms hold optional rewards.

Challenge

Freedom is controlled so the design can still speak. The sphere breaks, aims in only eight directions, and is switched off in challenge rooms. Some enemies demand a specific tool, and rewards are locked behind objectives.

5. Target Audience

Players who enjoy metroidvanias like Metroid, Castlevania and Bloodstained: Ritual of the Night, and movement-focused action games like Velocity 2X.

6. Inspirations

Metroid Ability-gated exploration of one connected map

7. The Design Problem

Teleportation is not a new idea. The design problem is freedom.

If the player can teleport often and at any time, they can skip a level completely. Ordinary platforming challenges stop meaning anything, so the level design has to be much more specific than usual.

The project fails if it loses the balance between freedom and design. The player must feel free, but that freedom has to be controlled to an extent that still lets the design speak.

8. How Freedom Is Controlled

Limit What it does
One sphere at a time No spamming, and it's always clear where you will teleport
The sphere is fragile It breaks on the first wall, so it can't bounce around corners to reach anywhere
8-direction aim Throws must be lined up; alignment matters
Challenge rooms The sphere doesn't work at all inside them
Locked rewards Most rewards sit behind keys or objectives, such as defeating every enemy
Fewer platforming rewards Platforming alone is easy to teleport past, so it's rarely what guards a reward
Tool-specific enemies and walls Some obstacles only give way to one ability

End-game power as the reward. In the full game the player starts without the TPS and gains everything gradually. Near the end they can skip whatever they want, and that is the reward for playing and finishing the game, which suits a metroidvania. The demo shows that final form.

9. Story and Setting

Namir is a jet pilot, one of the best in his planet's air defence.

His planet's scientists built a weapon: the TPS, a teleportation sphere. A group of terrorists heard about it and attacked. They destroyed the planet but could not find the TPS. The scientific department asked the air defence for help, and Namir and his squad were sent to retrieve the TPS before the terrorists.

They fought the terrorists in the sky. Namir's whole squad was wiped out, his jet was damaged, and he crashed into the desert where the demo begins.

The name. Namir means tiger in Arabic. In many mythologies, the Chinese ones for example, the tiger is a guardian. Namir is the guardian of the sky, which is why the enemy who killed his squad and brought down his jet is a dragon.

The mechanic. At the crash site, a mechanic is sealed behind fallen rock. The crash brought half the cliff down on him. One of the jet's parts landed next to him.

10. Core Loop — High Level

  1. Explore the map with the sphere and your sword.
  2. Hit a wall you can't pass, such as a sandfall, a cracked wall, ice, lava or a layer door.
  3. Remember it and keep exploring.
  4. Find a new ability or upgrade: a sphere type, a layer, or a sword.
  5. Return and open the way. The map changes under you.
  6. Collect the jet parts, repair the jet, and fly to the next region in a shoot-'em-up.

11. Core Loop — Moment to Moment

  1. Read the room. Find the gaps, the hazards, the enemies and the walls the sphere will hit.
  2. Aim. Hold right-click; the aim snaps to one of 8 directions.
  3. Throw. The sphere flies in a straight line.
  4. Decide while it flies. Teleport to it, let it break, cancel it, or (for the bomb) blow it up.
  5. Land and fight. Use the sword thrust and switch swords or spheres as the room asks.

In the designer's words: teleport anywhere you want.

13. The TPS (Teleportation Sphere)

Rules

Rule Value
Spheres in play 1
Aim Hold right-click; snaps to 8 directions
Throw speed 580 px/s, in a straight line (no gravity)
Lifetime 3 s
On hitting a wall Breaks, unless it has a layer to spend
Teleport Any time while it's flying; the warp takes 0.26 s
Recharge 0.3 s after a teleport, 0.5 s after a break
Cancel Middle click
Hitting an enemy 1 damage, and the sphere is destroyed

Why these rules

  • Why it breaks on the first wall. In the story, the TPS is fragile. In play, this stops the sphere bouncing around corners to reach anywhere, and it gives layers something to upgrade.
  • Why one sphere. With three spheres out, which one would you teleport to? The logical answer, the first one, makes the other two useless. Several spheres on screen would also be confusing, especially in combat.
  • Why separate throw and teleport buttons. The sticky wheel needs you to keep holding the aim button while you steer with WASD, so one shared button wouldn't work.
  • Why 8 directions. Free aim would be overpowered. Eight directions give alignment a purpose and feel good in platforming.
  • Why a hit destroys the sphere. The first concept was an arrow. It dealt damage whether you teleported or not, so players could stay far away and kill everything. In Warpbound an attack costs you the move.

Landing rule

You arrive centred on the sphere if there's room. If there isn't, you need two empty tiles stacked vertically: the sphere's own tile plus the one above or below it. You're fitted into that column, as close to the sphere as possible. This keeps teleporting smooth and fair.

What it does that a jump can't

It reaches places far beyond a jump: across wide gaps, over hazards, and up to ledges.

14. Layers

Each layer lets the sphere survive one more contact: a bounce off a wall, or a pass through a sandfall. Without a layer, the next contact breaks it.

Sphere Base layers Upgrades in the demo Maximum in the demo
Normal 1 → 3 with the Matryoshka upgrade +1 layer × 3 6
Sticky wheel 1 +1 layer × 1 (hidden) 2
Bomb 1 +1 layer × 3 4

Why layers. They let rooms be built for an exact number of layers. A room with four sandfalls and an upgrade behind them can't be crossed with three layers, so the player remembers it and comes back with four. That's a typical metroidvania room.

Why rings. Layers are drawn as rings around the sphere rather than making it bigger. A growing sphere would eventually need collision of its own. The normal sphere's rings are golden, so a fully upgraded sphere turns gold.

Why separate layers per type. They're different spheres. It wouldn't make sense for upgrading one to upgrade the others.

15. Sphere Types

Switch spheres with E.

Normal sphere (gold)

The default. It bounces using layers, passes through sandfalls by spending layers, and teleports you.

Sticky wheel (green)

Sticks to the surface it hits. Hold right-click and steer with WASD to roll it along surfaces at 160 px/s: up walls, round corners and across ceilings. It breaks on contact with enemies or spikes, because at its core it's still a TPS.

Why: it opens up puzzles where bouncing isn't useful, and spaces where you have to wait for enemies to jump or move before you can go on.

Bomb sphere (red)

Press the teleport key to detonate it instead of teleporting.

Property Value
Blast radius 64 px (2 tiles)
Damage 3
Breaks Cracked walls, crumbly floor, a guardian's guard
Goes off by itself No. Detonating when it runs out of layers is switched off

Why: the bomb is both an attack and a puzzle solver. It's the arrow problem split in two: the player can have damage and teleporting, but never in a single item. It only goes off when you press the button, like C4.

16. The Sword

Property Value
Attack Straight thrust toward the cursor, snapped to 8 directions
Thrust duration 0.2 s
Cooldown 0.34 s
Reach 52 px
Damage 2

Why a thrust, not a slash. The first version swung in an arc. The thrust replaced it because players should be precise rather than spam the attack button. Direction adds a dimension to combat, and positioning starts to matter.

Elemental swords

Switch swords with Q.

Sword Effect on the world Can't affect
Fire sword Melts ice into water Permafrost
Ice sword Freezes lava into solid rock, and water into ice Very hot lava

Why. It's common sense: ice and fire should affect the environment. It rewards the player for thinking logically. Permafrost and very hot lava let rooms say "not with this sword".

17. Enemies

Enemy Behaviour How to beat it
Turret Fixed; fires shots Sword or sphere
Eater Ground enemy; swallows your sphere Your sword only stuns it. A turret shot, a falling spike or a bomb kills it
Absorber Its field (84 px) catches the sphere and spirals it in; you can't teleport out Avoid its field, or set a bomb off inside it
Brute Walks at you and winds up a punch; only the punch hurts Sword hits stagger it and interrupt the punch
Flyer Hovers and drifts A sphere hit or a turret shot knocks it out of the air for 3 s
Guardian Its guard blocks everything, and spheres glance off it A bomb blast breaks the guard, then attack

Why some enemies ignore the sword. To make players use their items in different ways. Forcing the use of a particular ability is a common design tool. The eater turns enemies against each other: bait the turret into shooting it.

18. Bosses

The Hedgehog (R8)

He fires three spines, then charges to a column of cracked ceiling spikes and parks right under it. The quicksand in his arena slows the charge. The player shoots the spikes down onto him. Three hits.

He's the exam for R6, where the player first learns that cracked spikes can be knocked down.

Planned change: a falling spike will stun him, and the player then finishes him with the sword.

The Slugger (R21)

The sphere bounces, so the fight is tennis. He bats your sphere straight back at you, and a returned sphere comes back to your hand.

  • A bomb knocks his guard down for a few seconds. It never hurts him, and does nothing while he's already dazed.
  • Only the sword hurts him, and only while his guard is down.
  • His room holds a jet part behind a gate that opens when he's beaten.

19. Environment and Tiles

Tile Rule
Sandfall Blocks the player. The sphere passes through by spending a layer
Quicksand Slows and sinks the player
Cracked ceiling spike Knock it down with a sphere; it falls and becomes a floor, a weapon, or a hazard
Crumbly floor Collapses 0.6 s after you stand on it and returns after 3 s. Bombs smash it at once
Plank Can be rotated. You can jump up through it from below
Mini wall Plank-sized and rotatable, but solid from every side
Cracked wall Only a bomb breaks it. It stays broken for good
Ice / permafrost Ice melts to the fire sword; permafrost doesn't
Lava / very hot lava Lava freezes to the ice sword; very hot lava doesn't
Water Swim; the ice sword freezes it
Floor spikes Hurt the player and break the sticky wheel
Locked door + key Each door has its own key, which can be in another room
Layer door Opens when a sphere with at least the required number of layers hits it
Gate Opens when the room's boss dies
Hidden layer Any tile can hide another one behind it, revealed when the front tile is destroyed

Why mini walls as well as planks. Their collision is different: you can jump through a plank, but a mini wall is fully solid.

Why bombed walls stay broken. It's just logic. A wall you blew up doesn't rebuild itself.

Why the hidden layer. It rewards exploration. Something appearing behind a destroyed wall should feel like surprise mixed with accomplishment.

How rewards are placed. Each room is designed around one specific objective first. Once the rooms exist, rewards are distributed across the map and balanced.

21. Challenge Rooms and Layer Doors

Challenge rooms switch the sphere off completely, so the player has only their platforming and sword. They answer the freedom problem directly: inside them, nothing can be skipped.

Layer doors gate side rooms by sphere strength. Each door can require a different number of layers. The door in R2, right at the start of the game, needs 6, a fully upgraded normal sphere, and leads to the challenge room R20.

Side rooms always hold a reward, but they're never a mandatory objective.

22. The Crash Site and the Jet

The game opens at Namir's crashed jet in R1. The jet is there because the player will use it: once the three missing parts are found, it flies.

Part How you get it
1 A favour. The mechanic is sealed behind a diamond of cracked walls next to the wreck. Blow them open with the bomb sphere, and he gives you the part that landed beside him
2 Behind a locked door at the end of a lava room (R13). The key is in R12
3 Behind the Slugger's gate (R21), reached through a cracked floor in R1

Why a favour for the first part. It tries a different approach to the same problem. The three parts come from a favour, a locked door and a boss.

With all three parts, the player boards the jet with W and the flight begins.

23. The Jet Sequence (Shoot-'em-up)

Why a shoot-'em-up

From the start, the game has two kinds of sequences: the metroidvania map and shoot-'em-up sections. In the full game, the jet is how the player travels between biomes (Section 36). The current equipment decides the beams on the jet.

Rules

Beams Normal, Ice, Fire. They mirror the two elemental swords; in the full game, equipment decides which beams you have

Each has 3 health by default. Every enemy is hurt by only one beam, so the player reads each enemy's element and switches beams constantly. The swords carry their logic into the sky.

24. Fast-Travel Capsules

Capsules sit in R1, R4A and R9. A capsule starts dead. Throw a sphere into it to power it, then press W at any powered capsule to travel to another.

In the full game, a fast-travel machine waits in each new biome, so the player never has to replay a flight just to get back.

Structure

  • Main path: R1 → R2 → R3 → R4 → R5 → R6 → R7 → R8 (Hedgehog) → R9 (Matryoshka).
  • Sandfall branch: back to R4 with the Matryoshka → R4A → R12 → R13 (fire sword, jet part).
  • Upper branch from R5: R11 → R16 (ice sword) → R18 (sticky wheel), and R11 → R15 (bomb sphere) → R22.
  • Bomb-gated rooms: R10 and R19 behind R9's cracked walls; R21 (Slugger) under R1; the mechanic in R1.
  • Late side room: R20, behind R2's 6-layer door.

26. Level Design Principles

  • One objective per room. Each room is built around one specific idea; rewards are distributed and balanced afterwards.
  • Teach by failure, without killing. R3 has no text. The first gap clears with a jump and the second doesn't, so the player has to throw and teleport. Falling short drops you into quicksand, which gives you time to climb out, so it's fair for a new player.
  • Ability gates. You need an ability to move forward, as in R4's sandfall, which only a Matryoshka sphere survives.
  • Remember and return. Rooms built for a specific number of layers are meant to be remembered and revisited.
  • Combine tools. The strongest rooms make the player switch quickly between sphere types. R10, which combines two types of TPS with quick swapping, is the designer's favourite room.
  • Optional side rooms. Always a reward, never mandatory.
  • Reward exploring. The hidden layer turns destroyed walls into surprises.
  • Logic first. The rules follow common sense: ice freezes, fire melts, blown-up walls stay blown up, and a bomb waits for its button.

28. Camera and Screen

  • An 800 × 480 view with 32 px tiles.
  • Each normal room fills exactly one screen, so the sphere is always visible and every throw can be read at a glance.
  • Rooms connect through exits on their edges. A map (M / Tab) shows the rooms you've visited.

29. Difficulty and Failure

  • No game over. Dying restarts the current room with full health.
  • Fatal hazards: lava, very hot lava, sinking fully into quicksand, and falling out of the room.
  • In the jet: being shot down retries the current wave.

Why. The demo should feel fun to replay, and players should want to keep playing with the power-ups. Punishment would work against that.

30. Tutorial Philosophy

  • One prompt at a time. The tutorial shows one action and moves on as soon as the player has done it, even if they did it early.
  • Two sets: basics first (move, jump, sword thrust), then the sphere once the player picks it up (aim, throw, teleport, and "it breaks when it hits a wall").
  • Rooms teach the rest. R3 teaches the throw-and-teleport gap by failure, and R6 teaches knocking spikes down before the Hedgehog tests it.
  • The jet has its own tips for flying and beams.
  • Tutorial hints can be turned off in Options.

31. HUD and Interface

  • Hearts, top left.
  • Gear panel: the current sword, the current sphere with its layer rings, and jet parts collected.
  • Toasts: short messages for pickups and events, such as "Jet part 2 / 3" or "Guard broken!".
  • Dialogue box at the top of the screen, so it never covers the speaker.
  • Map on M / Tab.

32. Game Flow and Menus

Title: Continue (when a save exists) · New game · Controls · Options

Pause (on foot): Resume · Restart room · Controls · Options · Main menu

Pause (in the jet): Resume · Restart this wave · Leave the flight · Controls · Options · Main menu

Options: Full screen · Tutorial hints · Screen shake · Sound effects volume

Saving: the game autosaves every time you enter a room.

Flow of the demo: Crash site → find the TPS → explore and gather abilities → help the mechanic and find the other two jet parts → repair the jet → the flight → "Well done" → land back at the crash site, free to keep exploring.

33. Visual Direction

  • The art is placeholder, built for readability while the design settles.
  • Colour carries meaning. The normal sphere is gold, the sticky wheel green and the bomb red. Fire is orange and ice is blue, on the swords, the beams and the jet enemies.
  • Layers are shown as rings around the sphere, so its strength reads at a glance. A fully upgraded normal sphere turns gold.
  • Readable hazards: cracked walls glow in their cracks, and the bomb sphere blinks and sparks.

34. Audio Direction

Every sound in the demo is made in code with Web Audio: no audio files and no music. Footsteps change with the surface (sand, stone, wood, gravel, ice), sounds are panned by where they happen in the room, and held sounds loop for as long as they last: the jet engine, quicksand dragging you down, the sticky wheel rolling, and a different beam sound for each element. Options has a volume for sound effects.

35. Tools

Warpbound was built with its own level editor and wave editor, so every idea could be tested quickly.

Level editor

  • Paint rooms tile by tile from a palette of every tile, enemy, pickup and boss.
  • Link rooms through their exits, and pair doors with keys, even across rooms.
  • Set layer-door requirements, hidden tiles behind tiles, background blocks, plank and mini-wall angles, and challenge-room status.
  • Write NPC dialogue and per-room design notes.
  • Tune the game's numbers (sphere speed, recharge, jump, bomb radius and more) and lay out the HUD.
  • Import custom tiles from images, borrowing an existing tile's behaviour.
  • Play any room instantly, and export a standalone build.

Wave editor (jet)

Choosing Edit asks whether to open the jet editor or the metroidvania editor. The wave editor follows the one from the designer's earlier shoot-'em-up.

  • Build waves enemy by enemy: type, entry side, movement or path, speed, health, fire rate and shot pattern (aimed, straight, spread, homing, laser or none).
  • Add rules such as "when the gunner falls, the rest enrage", "when one is left, they all dive" or "reinforcements".
  • Add solid terrain, moving terrain and doors.
  • Test-fly any wave instantly.

36. Full Game Vision

The demo is a slice of a larger game.

  • Five areas: a main area, the desert, snow, magma and a destroyed city.
  • Six bosses.
  • Gradual powers. The player starts without the TPS and earns its types and layers over the whole game. By the end they can skip whatever they want, and that's the reward for finishing.
  • Swords: a default sword, Fang of Hell (fire) and Glacier Shard (ice).
  • Flights between biomes. Travelling to a new biome, such as the lava or ice biome, is an actual shoot-'em-up level. The player's current equipment decides the jet's beams.
  • Fast-travel machines. Arriving in a new biome, the player finds a fast-travel machine, so the flight doesn't have to be replayed each time.
  • Replayable flights. Cleared flight levels unlock in the main menu, to replay as often as the player wants and chase better scores.

37. Demo Scope

In the demo

  • 22 rooms in the desert area.
  • All three sphere types, with layer upgrades.
  • Both elemental swords.
  • 6 enemy types and 2 bosses.
  • A jet repair quest with 3 parts, one through an NPC favour.
  • One 8-wave flight with element-locked beams.
  • 3 fast-travel capsules.
  • Tutorial, menus, options, map and autosave.
  • Level and wave editors.

Not in the demo yet

  • Final art.
  • The other areas and bosses.
  • Gradual acquisition of abilities (the demo hands them out quickly).
  • Flight replays and scores in the main menu.

38. Next Steps

  • Hedgehog: a falling spike stuns him, then the sword finishes him.
  • Rooms still in progress: R22 and R23 have no reward yet, and several rooms still carry placeholder names.

39. What Warpbound Should Never Become

  • A game where freedom erases the design. If teleporting lets the player skip every room, the level design has nothing left to say.
  • A game where one throw attacks and moves you for free. That's the arrow problem. An attack always costs the move.
  • A screen full of spheres. One sphere keeps every decision readable.
  • A free-aim shooter. Eight directions keep alignment meaningful.

40. Design Philosophy

  • Every power gets a limit. Freedom is the pillar, so each new freedom comes with a rule that keeps the rooms meaningful.
  • Split what's too strong. When one item does too much, divide its jobs between items, as with the sphere and the bomb.
  • Follow logic. Players should be able to predict the world: fire melts, ice freezes, broken walls stay broken.
  • Teach without punishing. Let the player fail, but don't kill them for learning.
  • Reward curiosity. Hidden tiles, side rooms and remembered gates.
  • Try more than one approach to the same problem.
  • Test ideas quickly. Build the tools that let an idea be played minutes after it's imagined.

41. Portfolio Value

Warpbound shows:

  • Systems design with clear reasoning: a core mechanic with explicit limits, and a reason behind every rule.
  • Level design: a connected 22-room map with ability gates, layer gates, optional rooms and hidden rewards.
  • Boss design built on the core mechanic, such as tennis with a bouncing sphere.
  • Genre blending: a shoot-'em-up whose rules come from the metroidvania's swords.
  • Tool design: a level editor and a wave editor made to support fast iteration.
  • Scope control: a demo that deliberately shows the end-game form of the player.

42. Final Summary

Warpbound is a metroidvania where you can teleport anywhere you want.

Its central challenge is freedom: giving players a movement power strong enough to skip a level, then designing the limits that keep every room meaningful. The fragile sphere, its layers, the three sphere types, the elemental swords, the challenge rooms and the flights between biomes all serve that one balance.

Freedom, exploration, challenge, and design that still speaks.
